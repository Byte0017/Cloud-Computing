# 1. What is ITIL?

## The Simple Definition

ITIL stands for Information Technology Infrastructure Library. In the simplest possible words:

## Layman Definition

ITIL is a collection of best-practice guidance that tells IT teams how to plan, deliver, support, and continually improve IT services so that they actually help the business and keep customers happy - instead of IT just "keeping servers running" in isolation.

Think of ITIL as a cookbook of proven recipes, not a strict rulebook. It does not tell you the exact tool to use or the exact software to buy. It tells you the ingredients and steps that experienced IT organizations, across thousands of companies over 30+ years, have found actually work. You still adapt the recipe to your own kitchen (your own company).

## Everyday Analogy

**Analogy: The Restaurant**

Imagine a restaurant with no standard recipes, no order-taking process, and no way to handle a customer complaint. Every chef cooks differently, waiters forget orders, and when food is bad, nobody knows whether it was the recipe, the ingredient, or the chef. ITIL is like giving that restaurant a proven operating manual: a standard way to take orders (Service Request Management), a standard way to handle a burnt dish complaint (Incident Management), a standard way to find out why dishes keep burning (Problem Management), and a standard way to safely change the menu (Change Enablement).

**What ITIL is NOT**
| Myth | Reality |
| :--- | :--- |
| ITIL is a certification only | Certification is one part; ITIL is primarily an operating framework for real work |
| ITIL is a strict law you must follow exactly | ITIL is guidance you adapt — "adopt and adapt," not "adopt as-is" |
| ITIL is only for large enterprises | Concepts scale down to small IT teams too |
| ITIL is a software product | ITIL is a framework; tools like ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, BMC Remedy implement ITIL processes |

**Key Terms You Must Know First**
| Term | Simple Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| Service | A way of helping someone get a job done, without them having to own all the cost and risk themselves. Example: email service — you use email without managing mail servers. |
| Utility | “Is it fit for PURPOSE?” — does the service do what is needed? (functionality) |
| Warranty | “Is it fit for USE?” — is it available, reliable, secure, and has enough capacity? (assurance) |
| Value | The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something to the customer |
| Stakeholder | Anyone with an interest in the service — customers, users, suppliers, IT staff |
| Outcome | The result that a stakeholder wants to achieve (not just an output/deliverable) |

## Common Fresher Confusion: Utility vs Warranty

A car has great utility if it can take you from home to office (the function works). But if it breaks down every 2 days, has no fuel efficiency, or is unsafe — it has poor warranty. A service needs BOTH to create value. A banking app that has every feature (utility) but crashes during month-end (poor warranty) still fails the customer.

## Scenario

TechNova Bank launches a mobile banking app.

- **Service =** Mobile Banking
- **Utility =** Customers can check balance, transfer money, pay bills
- **Warranty =** App is available 99.9% of the time, transactions are secure, app loads within 2 seconds
- **Value =** Customer saves a trip to the branch and feels in control of their money
- **Stakeholders =** Customers, bank operations team, IT support team, the regulator, the app vendor

If the app has all features but keeps crashing at month-end salary credit time, utility is fine but warranty has failed - and so has the overall value.

# History, Purpose & Evolution of ITIL

## Why ITIL Was Created (Purpose)

In the 1980s, the UK Government's CCTA (Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency) noticed that IT departments across government and industry were reinventing IT service practices independently - inconsistently, with wildly different quality. There was no shared standard for how IT should be delivered as a service. ITIL was created to capture and standardize the best practices that were already working well in various organizations, so everyone didn't have to learn the same lessons the hard way.

## Evolution Timeline

*[Diagram: Timeline with green and yellow dots]*

| Version | Year | What Changed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ITIL v1 | 1989-1996 | Published as 30+ separate books, each covering a specific IT process. Very detailed but hard to use as a whole. |
| ITIL v2 | 2000-2002 | Consolidated into fewer books, most famous being "Service Support" and "Service Delivery." First version to get wide global adoption. |
| ITIL v3 | 2007 | Introduced the Service Lifecycle approach: Strategy, Design, Transition, Operation, Continual Service Improvement (5 books). Much more business-focused. |
| ITIL 2011 | 2011 | An update/refresh of v3 fixing inconsistencies — not a major version, sometimes called "v3.1" |
| ITIL 4 | 2019 | Complete redesign: introduced the Service Value System, 4 Dimensions, and Guiding Principles. Built for Agile, DevOps, and Cloud-era IT — less rigid, more flexible and value-focused. |

## Why ITIL 4 Was a Big Shift

**The Core Change: Lifecycle → Value System**

ITIL v3 organized everything around a linear lifecycle (Strategy → Design → Transition → Operation → Improvement) which felt like a waterfall, one-direction flow. ITIL 4 replaced this with a flexible Service Value System where activities like Plan, Design, Build, Deliver, and Improve can happen in any order, looping back and forth — matching how modern Agile/DevOps teams actually work, where you release small changes constantly instead of one big yearly release.

## Who Owns ITIL Today

ITIL was originally owned by the UK Government (via CCTA, later OGC). Ownership passed to AXELOS (a joint venture between the UK Cabinet Office and Capita) in 2013, and in 2021 PeopleCert acquired AXELOS's ITIL and related best-practice product lines, including exam and accreditation management.

**Trainer Note:** Freshers often ask "do I need to remember all the exact years?" — for the ITIL Foundation exam, focus on the concept "ITIL evolved from rigid process books to a flexible value system," not memorizing every year. Give the timeline as context, not as rote-memorization material.

# What are Guiding Principles?

Guiding Principles are recommendations that guide an organization in all circumstances - regardless of changes in goals, strategies, or management structure. Think of them as universal wisdom / common-sense rules for good decision-making, similar to "measure twice, cut once" in carpentry. They apply to EVERY ITIL practice and every decision, not just one specific process.

*[Diagram: 7 Guiding Principles arranged in a circle around a purple center]*

## 1. Focus on Value
Everything the organization does must link back, directly or indirectly, to value for stakeholders. Before doing any work, ask: "Who is this for, and what value does it create for them?"

**Scenario:** An IT team spends 3 weeks building a beautifully engineered internal reporting dashboard nobody asked for, while a customer-facing login bug sits unfixed. This violates Focus on Value - effort was spent where no one was asking for value.

## 2. Start Where You Are
Don't discard existing services, processes, tools, or people just to start fresh. Assess what already exists and reuse/improve it if it can still create value. Measure before you redesign - you might be surprised what's already working.

**Scenario:** A new manager wants to scrap the existing (imperfect) incident-logging tool and build a new one from scratch immediately. Start Where You Are says: first measure how the current tool is actually performing, understand what's working, and improve incrementally - don't assume "new" always means "better."

## 3. Progress Iteratively with Feedback
Don't try to do everything in one giant initiative. Break work into small, manageable pieces, get feedback after each piece, and adjust. This is the same thinking behind Agile sprints.

## 4. Collaborate and Promote Visibility
Work across silos. Involve the right stakeholders at the right time. Make work, progress, and decisions visible to trust is built and blockages are spotted early. Visibility doesn't mean "share everything with everyone" — it means the right information reaches the right people.

**Scenario:** A Change Advisory Board (CAB) reviews upcoming production changes with representatives from Dev, Ops, Security, and the Business — instead of one team silently deciding and deploying alone.

## 5. Think and Work Holistically
No service stands alone. Every service is a combination of activities, processes, people, partners, technology, and information working together as a system. Fixing one part without considering the rest often just moves the problem elsewhere.

**Scenario:** Speeding up the deployment pipeline (technology) without training the release team (people) or updating the approval process (process) creates faster releases that break more often — because only one part of the system was improved.

## 6. Keep It Simple and Practical
If a process, report, or metric doesn't add value, remove it. Always use the minimum number of steps needed to accomplish an objective. Complexity should be justified, not assumed.

**Scenario:** A low-risk password reset request requires 5 levels of manager approval, copied from a process designed for high-risk infrastructure changes. Keep It Simple and Practical says: match the approval complexity to the actual risk of the request.

## 7. Optimize and Automate
Before automating anything, first simplify and optimize the underlying process (automating a broken process just makes mistakes happen faster). Use technology to reduce manual, repetitive work wherever it makes sense.

**Scenario:** Instead of automating an outdated 8-step manual approval chain exactly as it is, the team first reduces it to 3 necessary steps, THEN automates those 3 steps using a workflow tool.

## Important for Freshers & the Exam
The 7 principles are not a checklist you tick one at a time — in real situations, several apply simultaneously, and you weigh them against each other for the specific context. There is no fixed order of priority; the ITIL guidance explicitly says all should be considered together, though some may carry more weight in a given situation.

# The Four Dimensions of Service Management

## Why "Four Dimensions"?

When something goes wrong with a service, freshers often assume "it's a technology problem." ITIL 4 teaches that every service depends on four dimensions working together - if you only look at technology and ignore the other three, you'll keep missing the real cause of problems. Think of it as a 4-legged table: if any one leg is weak, the whole table wobbles.

*[Diagram: Purple 4-legged table with "Products & Services" in the center. The legs are the Four Dimensions. Surrounded by external PESTLE factors]*

## Dimension 1: Organizations and People
Covers the organization's structure, roles, responsibilities, culture, and the skills/competencies of the people involved. A service is only as good as the people running it and the culture they work in.

**Scenario:** A company buys the best monitoring tool in the market, but the operations team has never been trained on it and is understaffed for night shifts. The technology dimension is strong, but the Organizations and People dimension is weak - so incidents still go undetected for hours.

## Dimension 2: Information and Technology
Covers the technologies (applications, infrastructure, cloud platforms) AND the information/knowledge used to deliver services - including data quality, information security, and knowledge management.

**Scenario:** Two teams maintain separate, outdated spreadsheets listing "critical servers" with conflicting information. During an outage, engineers waste 20 minutes arguing over which list is correct. This is a failure in the Information and Technology dimension - specifically information quality, not the technology itself.

## Dimension 3: Partners and Suppliers
Covers relationships with other organizations involved in the design, development, deployment, delivery, support, and/or continual improvement of services - cloud providers, software vendors, outsourced support desks, telecom providers, etc.

**Scenario:** TechNova's e-commerce platform depends on a third-party payment gateway. If that gateway has an outage, TechNova's checkout fails — even though TechNova's own code and infrastructure are perfectly healthy. This is why supplier SLAs and dependency-mapping matter.

## Dimension 4: Value Streams and Processes
Covers how the organization's various parts work together in an integrated way to enable value creation through products and services — the actual workflows, activities, and process steps.

**Scenario:** A new employee's laptop request takes 3 weeks because it passes through 7 disconnected approval steps across 4 different teams who don't talk to each other. Excellent people, tools, and vendors don't help if the underlying process/value stream itself is broken.

## The Four Dimensions Are Interconnected

**Key Exam & Real-World Point:**
The four dimensions must be considered together, not in isolation. A change in one dimension almost always affects the others. Example: automating a manual approval process (Value Streams and Processes) may require new tooling (Information and Technology), retraining staff (Organizations and People), and renegotiating a vendor contract (Partners and Suppliers).

# PESTLE External Factors

Surrounding the four dimensions are external factors that the organization cannot control but must respond to. ITIL uses the well-known PESTLE framework to categorize these.

| Factor | Stands For | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| P | Political | Government IT policy, data sovereignty laws |
| E | Economic | Currency fluctuation affecting cloud costs billed in USD |
| S | Social | Rise of remote work changing how services must be delivered |
| T | Technological | Emergence of AI/cloud-native tools changing what's possible |
| L | Legal | Data protection laws like GDPR or India's DDPP Act |
| E | Environmental | Sustainability requirements, green data center mandates |

**Scenario:** A bank operating across India and Europe must design its data storage service differently for European customers due to GDPR (Legal), while also responding to customer expectations around carbon-neutral data centers (Environmental). These are external factors the four dimensions must adapt around — the bank can't change GDPR, but it must design its service to comply with it.

# Service Value System (SVS)

## Big Picture First

The Service Value System (SVS) describes how ALL the components and activities of an organization work together to enable value creation. It answers the question: "How does an idea, request, or opportunity turn into value for a customer?" It is the "big umbrella" that contains everything else you'll learn this week — Guiding Principles, Governance, the Service Value Chain, Practices, and Continual Improvement.

*[Diagram: SVS Box with Input arrows and Output arrows]*
- **Input:** Opportunity / Demand
- **Box Content:** Guiding Principles, Governance, Practices (34), Service Value Chain, Continual Improvement
- **Output:** Value

## Input and Output of the SVS
| Input | Output |
| :--- | :--- |
| Opportunity/Demand — a business need, a new market opportunity, or a customer requirement that could be fulfilled | Value — the benefit realized by the organization, its customers, and other stakeholders |

## The 5 Components of the SVS
| Component | What it Does |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Guiding Principles** | The 7 principles you learned on Day 2 — universal recommendations for decision-making |
| **Governance** | The means by which an organization is directed and controlled (who sets policy, who is accountable) |
| **Service Value Chain** | The set of 6 interconnected activities that create value (covered in Module 4.2 today) |
| **Practices** | The 34 sets of organizational resources for performing work (covered Day 5) — e.g. Incident Management, Change Enablement |
| **Continual Improvement** | A recurring activity performed at all levels to ensure the organization's performance keeps meeting stakeholder expectations |

## Common Confusion: SVS vs Service Value Chain

Freshers often mix these up. The SVS is the whole system (all 5 components above). The Service Value Chain is just ONE of those 5 components — specifically, the operating model showing the key activities. Think of SVS as the entire factory, and the Service Value Chain as the production line inside it.

# The Service Value Chain (SVC)

The Service Value Chain is an operating model that outlines the key activities required to respond to demand and facilitate value creation through the creation and management of products and services. The 6 activities can be combined in many different sequences depending on the situation - there is no single fixed order.

*[Diagram: 6 Value Chain Activities arranged with "IMPROVE" in the center surrounding every activity]*

| Activity | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Plan** | Ensure a shared understanding of vision, current status, and improvement direction across the organization |
| **Improve** | Ensure continual improvement of products, services, and practices across every value chain activity |
| **Engage** | Provide a good understanding of stakeholder needs, transparency, and good relationships with all stakeholders |
| **Design & Transition** | Ensure products and services meet stakeholder expectations for quality, cost, and time-to-market |
| **Obtain/Build** | Ensure service components are available when and where needed, meeting agreed specifications |
| **Deliver & Support** | Ensure services are delivered and supported according to agreed specifications and stakeholder expectations |

## Scenario: New Feature Request Flows Through the Value Chain

TechNova's customers request a "forgot PIN" self-service feature for the mobile banking app.
- **Engage:** Customer support and product team gather the requirement from customer feedback
- **Plan:** The request is prioritized against the roadmap and budget
- **Design & Transition:** The feature is designed, developed, and tested before release
- **Obtain/Build:** Any new components (e.g., SMS OTP gateway integration) are procured or built
- **Deliver & Support:** The feature goes live; Service Desk is briefed to support user queries
- **Improve:** Usage data is reviewed after 1 month; UI tweaks are made based on drop-off patterns

Notice these activities are not always sequential - "Improve" activities can trigger new "Plan" or "Engage" work, looping back continuously.

# ITIL Practices

## What is a "Practice"?

A Practice is a set of organizational resources (people, process, tools, information) designed for performing a specific type of work or accomplishing a specific objective. ITIL 4 defines 34 practices in total, grouped into 3 practice groups. (Note: ITIL v3 called these "processes" — ITIL 4 calls them "practices" because a practice is broader than just a process; it includes people, tools, and capability, not just workflow steps.)

*[Diagram: 3 Boxes representing the Practice Groups]*
- **General Management Practices: 14** (Risk, Knowledge, Supplier, Financial Management etc.)
- **Service Management Practices: 17** (Incident, Problem, Change, Service Desk, Service Request etc.)
- **Technical Management Practices: 3** (Infrastructure, Software Dev, Deployment)

## 34 Total ITIL Practices

### Group 1: General Management Practices (14)
Practices adopted and adapted from general business management for the purpose of service management. Not IT-specific — used across the whole organization.
- Strategy Management

- Architecture Management
- Workforce and Talent Management
- Continual Improvement
- Measurement and Reporting
- Risk Management
- Information Security Management
- Knowledge Management
- Organizational Change Management
- Project Management
- Relationship Management
- Supplier Management
- Financial Management for Services

### Group 2: Service Management Practices (17)
Practices developed specifically for service management and service delivery contexts.
- Business Analysis
- Service Catalogue Management
- Service Design
- Service Level Management
- Availability Management
- Capacity and Performance Management
- Service Continuity Management
- Monitoring and Event Management
- **Incident Management ★**
- **Problem Management ★**
- **Change Enablement ★**
- **Service Request Management ★**
- **Service Desk ★**
- Service Validation and Testing
- Release Management
- Deployment Management

## ⭐ Today's Focus: The 6 Key Practices
Out of 34 practices, this course focuses deeply on the 6 that a fresher will encounter almost daily in any IT job: Incident Management, Problem Management, Change Enablement, Service Request Management, Service Desk, and Continual Improvement. These form the backbone of day-to-day IT operations.

# 1. Incident Management

## Purpose
To minimize the negative impact of incidents by restoring normal service operation as quickly as possible. An Incident is an unplanned interruption to a service, or a reduction in the quality of a service.

## Key Idea: Speed Over Perfection
Incident Management is NOT about finding the root cause — that's Problem Management's job (next topic). Incident Management's only job is to get the service working again as fast as possible, even with a temporary workaround.

## Typical Incident Lifecycle
1. Identification — incident is detected (by monitoring tool or reported by a user)
2. Logging — recorded with details, timestamp, affected service
3. Categorization — classified by type (e.g., network, application, hardware)
4. Prioritization — based on Impact × Urgency
5. Diagnosis & Resolution — workaround or fix is applied
6. Closure — confirmed with the user, incident record closed

**Priority = Impact × Urgency**

| | Urgency: High | Urgency: Medium | Urgency: Low |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Impact: High** | Priority 1 (Critical) | Priority 2 (High) | Priority 3 (Medium) |
| **Impact: Medium** | Priority 2 (High) | Priority 3 (Medium) | Priority 4 (Low) |
| **Impact: Low** | Priority 3 (Medium) | Priority 4 (Low) | Priority 4 (Low) |

*Impact = how many users/business processes are affected. Urgency = how quickly the incident needs resolving before serious harm occurs.*

**Scenario:** A single employee's printer isn't working (Low Impact, Low Urgency = Priority 4). Compare that to the entire company's email server going down during a product launch announcement (High Impact, High Urgency = Priority 1/Critical). Both are "incidents," but they get very different treatment and response times.

## Major Incident
A Major Incident is an incident with significant business impact requiring a response beyond routine handling — usually involves a separate, faster procedure: a dedicated major incident manager, a bridge call with all relevant teams, and frequent stakeholder communication until resolved.

# 2. Problem Management

## Purpose
To reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents by identifying actual and potential causes, and managing workarounds and known errors. A Problem is the underlying cause (or potential cause) of one or more incidents.

## Three Phases of Problem Management
| Phase | What Happens |
| :--- | :--- |
| Problem Identification | Spotting problems, often from patterns in repeated incidents, or from proactive analysis of trends |
| Problem Control | Root cause analysis; documenting workarounds; creating a Known Error record |
| Error Control | Managing known errors until a permanent fix is implemented (usually via Change Enablement) |

## Known Error Database (KEDB)
A Known Error is a problem that has been analyzed but not yet permanently resolved. It is documented in a Known Error Database (KEDB) along with its workaround, so that when the same underlying issue causes future incidents, the Service Desk can apply the known workaround immediately instead of re-investigating from scratch.

## Scenario Connecting Incident & Problem
Over 3 weeks, the Service Desk logs 12 separate Incidents about "VPN disconnects randomly after 30 minutes." Each time, the workaround is "reconnect the VPN." A Problem record is opened to investigate WHY this keeps happening. Root cause analysis finds an idle-timeout misconfiguration on the VPN concentrator. This becomes a Known Error with a documented workaround (reconnect) while a permanent fix (reconfigure timeout setting) is submitted as a Change.

*[Diagram: Incident (VPN drop) -> Problem Record (root cause investigation) -> Known Error (workaround documented) -> Change Enablement (permanent fix deployed)]*

## Common Fresher Confusion: Incident vs Problem
- **Incident =** "The service is broken right now, fix it fast" (reactive, urgent).
- **Problem =** "Why does this keep breaking, and how do we stop it forever" (investigative, root-cause focused).

One Problem can cause MANY Incidents. Fixing the Problem (usually via a Change) is what finally stops the Incidents from recurring.

# 3. Change Enablement

## Purpose
To maximize the number of successful IT changes by ensuring risks are properly assessed, authorizing changes to proceed, and managing a change schedule. Note: ITIL 4 renamed this from "Change Management" (v3) to "Change Enablement" to emphasize that the goal is to ENABLE beneficial change quickly and safely — not to slow everything down with bureaucracy.

## Three Types of Change
| Type | Description | Approval |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Standard Change** | Low-risk, pre-authorized, well-understood, and follows a defined procedure (e.g., resetting a password) | Pre-approved; no CAB needed each time |
| **Normal Change** | Needs to be scheduled, assessed, and authorized following a defined process (e.g., a new application deployment) | Change Authority / CAB review |
| **Emergency Change** | Must be implemented as soon as possible (e.g., emergency security patch for an active vulnerability) | Expedited approval process, often a smaller emergency-CAB |

## Change Advisory Board (CAB)
A group of people who assess, prioritize, authorize, and schedule changes. Typically includes representatives from IT, security, business, and sometimes external suppliers — supporting the "Collaborate and Promote Visibility" guiding principle.

**Scenario:** TechNova needs to patch a critical security vulnerability discovered in production today (Emergency Change — fast-tracked approval). Next month they plan a major upgrade of their core banking application (Normal Change — goes through full CAB review, testing, and scheduled maintenance window). Meanwhile, resetting a locked user account follows a pre-approved script every time (Standard Change — no CAB needed).

## Real-World Trap
Treating every change like a Normal Change (heavy CAB process) slows the business down and violates "Keep It Simple and Practical." Treating every change like a Standard Change (no review) is reckless and increases outage risk. Good Change Enablement means correctly classifying each change.

# 4. Service Request Management

## Purpose
To support the agreed quality of a service by handling all pre-defined, user-initiated service requests in an effective and user-friendly manner. A Service Request is a request from a user (or their representative) that initiates a service action which has been agreed as a normal part of service delivery.

## Critical Distinction: Service Request vs Incident

**Common Fresher Confusion**
- A **Service Request** is something the user is ASKING FOR that is normal, planned, low-risk work (e.g., "please give me access to the shared drive," "please install MS Office on my laptop," "please reset my password").
- An **Incident** is something BROKEN that shouldn't be happening (e.g., "my laptop won't turn on," "I can't log in even though my credentials are correct").
- Trick question: "I forgot my password" — is this an Incident or a Service Request? It's usually a Service Request, because password reset is a normal, pre-agreed, expected action — not something "broken."

## Types of Service Requests
| Type | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Request for a service action | "Please back up my laptop before I travel" |
| Request for information | "How do I connect to the VPN?" |
| Request for access to a resource/service | "Give me access to the shared finance folder" |
| Feedback, compliments, or complaints | "The new self-service portal is confusing" |

**Scenario:** A new employee joins TechNova. Their manager submits requests for: a laptop, email account, VPN access, and access to the CRM tool. All four are Service Requests — pre-approved, catalogued, standard onboarding items — handled through a self-service portal with automated workflows, not treated as urgent "incidents."

# 5. Service Desk

## Purpose
To capture demand for incident resolution and service requests — the Service Desk is the entry point and single point of contact for the service provider with all of its users.

## Why the Service Desk Matters More Than People Think
The Service Desk is often the ONLY human interaction a user has with the entire IT organization. A user doesn't judge IT by how good the backend infrastructure is — they judge IT by how their call/chat/ticket with the Service Desk was handled. This is why "soft skills" (empathy, clear communication) are as important as technical skills for Service Desk staff.

## Service Desk Channels
| Channel | Notes |
| :--- | :--- |
| Phone | Still critical for urgent/major incidents |
| Email | Good for non-urgent, trackable requests |
| Self-service Portal | Users log tickets themselves, check status, browse a knowledge base |
| Chat / Chatbot | Increasingly common for quick queries and initial triage |
| Walk-up / In-person | Common in campus or manufacturing environments |

## Service Desk Models
| Model | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| Local Service Desk | Co-located with the users it serves |
| Centralized Service Desk | One physical or logical location serving the whole organization |
| Virtual Service Desk | Staff distributed across locations, appearing as a single service to users through technology |
| Follow-the-Sun | Multiple geographically distributed desks handing off across time zones for 24x7 coverage |

# 6. Continual Improvement

## Purpose
To align the organization's practices and services with changing business needs through ongoing identification and improvement of services, products, and practices at every level. Note: this is both a Guiding Component of the SVS AND a standalone Practice - it's important enough to be both.

## The Continual Improvement Model (7 Steps)
1. **What is the vision?** - link improvement to business goals
2. **Where are we now?** - baseline assessment
3. **Where do we want to be?** - define measurable targets
4. **How do we get there?** - define the improvement plan
5. **Take action** - execute the plan
6. **Did we get there?** - measure and evaluate results
7. **How do we keep the momentum going?** - embed and sustain the improvement

**Scenario:** TechNova notices average incident resolution time is 6 hours (Step 2: Where are we now?). Business wants it under 2 hours for critical incidents to meet a new SLA with a key client (Step 1 & 3: Vision and Target). The team introduces automated alerting and a revised escalation matrix (Step 4 & 5: Plan and Act). After 2 months, average resolution time drops to 1.5 hours (Step 6: Did we get there?). The new escalation matrix is written into the standard operating procedure so it doesn't slip back (Step 7: Sustain).

## Key Takeaway
Continual Improvement is not a one-time project - it is meant to be a habit built into daily work, at every level from front-line Service Desk staff to senior leadership, using the same 7-step logic every time.

# Glossary of Key ITIL Terms

| Term | Definition |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Service** | A means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes customers want, without the customer managing specific costs and risks |
| **Utility** | The functionality offered by a product or service to meet a particular need (“fit for purpose”) |
| **Warranty** | Assurance that a product or service will meet agreed requirements (“fit for use”) |
| **Value** | The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something |
| **Outcome** | A result for a stakeholder enabled by one or more outputs |
| **Stakeholder** | Any person or organization with an interest in a service |
| **Guiding Principle** | A recommendation that guides decision-making in all circumstances |
| **Governance** | The means by which an organization is directed and controlled |
| **Service Value System (SVS)** | Describes how all components/activities work together to enable value creation |
| **Service Value Chain** | Operating model outlining the key activities to respond to demand and create value |
| **Practice** | A set of organizational resources for performing work or accomplishing an objective |
| **Incident** | An unplanned interruption to a service or reduction in quality of a service |
| **Problem** | A cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents |
| **Known Error** | A problem that has been analyzed but not permanently resolved |
| **Change** | The addition, modification, or removal of anything that could affect services |
| **Service Request** | A user-initiated request for a pre-agreed, normal service action |
| **CAB** | Change Advisory Board — group that assesses and authorizes changes |
| **KEDB** | Known Error Database — repository of documented known errors and workarounds |
| **SLA** | Service Level Agreement — documented agreement of expected service levels |
| **Major Incident** | An incident with significant business impact requiring an expedited response process |
